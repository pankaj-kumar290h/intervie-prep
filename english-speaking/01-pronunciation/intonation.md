# Intonation (the melody of English)

Intonation = the rise and fall of your pitch across a sentence. It carries meaning
that the words don't: whether you're finished, asking, listing, doubting, or
contrasting. A flat monotone makes you sound bored, unsure, or rude even when your
words are perfect.

---

## The 4 patterns you actually need

### 1. Falling – statements, commands, and "wh-" questions
Pitch goes **down** at the end. Signals certainty and "I'm done."

- "I work as a software engineer. �“"
- "Close the door. ↓"
- "Where do you live? ↓"
- "What time is the meeting? ↓"

Learners often rise here, which makes every statement sound like a question or like
they're unsure. Commit to the fall.

### 2. Rising – yes/no questions and "checking"
Pitch goes **up** at the end.

- "Are you coming? ↑"
- "Do you have a minute? ↑"
- "Really? ↑"
- "You mean the second one? ↑"

### 3. Rise then fall (or fall–rise) – "list" and "not finished"
Rise on each item, fall on the last.

- "We need milk ↑, eggs ↑, bread ↑, and butter ↓."
- "First I opened the file ↑, then I ran the tests ↑, and finally I deployed ↓."

Fall–rise (down then slightly up) signals reservation / "but...":
- "The food was *nice* ↓↑..." (implying: but something wasn't)
- "I *can* do it ↓↑..." (implying: but not today)

### 4. Emphasis / contrast – jump the pitch on the key word
- "I said the *blue* one, not the *green* one."
- "It's not *my* problem, it's *yours*."
- "That was *amazing*."

---

## Tone units – chunk your speech

Break long sentences into thought-groups with a small pause and pitch reset at each
`/`. This gives listeners time and gives you time to breathe/think.

> "When I joined the team / about two years ago / we didn't have any tests / so the
> first thing I did / was set up a CI pipeline."

Practice: mark the chunks in a paragraph, then read it grouping them clearly.

---

## Sounding engaged (not monotone)

- **Widen your pitch range.** Learners speak in a narrow band. Deliberately go
  higher on stressed content words and lower at the end of statements. Exaggerate
  in practice; it settles down in real speech.
- **Step down through a sentence.** English statements tend to drift downward
  overall, with the biggest fall on the last stressed word.
- **Key word gets the peak.** Decide which one word matters most in your sentence
  and put your highest pitch + most length there.

---

## Politeness through intonation

Same words, different vibe:

- "What do you want?" – flat/falling hard = aggressive.
- "What do you want?" – gentle rise-fall, wider range = friendly.
- "Can you send me the file?" – flat = order. Slight rise + softer = request.

Add "could you", "would you mind", and a final rise for requests at work.

---

## Drills

1. **Question sort.** Write 20 questions (10 yes/no, 10 wh-). Say each with the
   correct rise/fall. Record and check.
2. **The "really?" game.** Say "really?" to mean: surprised, doubtful, bored,
   delighted, angry. Five different melodies, same word.
3. **Shadowing with a hum.** Play a line, hum only its melody (no words), then say
   it matching that melody.
4. **Read a children's story aloud** with full expression. Over-act it. This
   stretches your pitch range fast.
5. **Record a 1-min monologue.** Listen only for melody: where did you go flat?
   Where did you rise when you should have fallen? Redo it.
