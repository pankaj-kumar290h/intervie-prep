# Thinking in English

The biggest hidden slowdown is **translating**: you form the sentence in Hindi, then
convert it word by word. This is slow *and* produces unnatural English (word order,
articles, collocations, phrasal verbs all come out wrong). The fix is to build a
direct Hindi-free channel: situation → English, no detour.

You can't force this by willpower. You build it with specific habits over weeks.

---

## Why translation fails

- **Speed:** two mental steps instead of one. You'll always lag.
- **Structure:** Hindi is Subject-Object-Verb; English is Subject-Verb-Object.
  Word-for-word gives "I bread eat".
- **Idiom:** "मुझे नींद आ रही है" → "Sleep is coming to me" instead of "I'm sleepy."
- **Chunks:** Hindi has different collocations, so you get "do a mistake", "open the
  light".

The goal isn't "never use Hindi" – it's making English the *default* for everyday
thought.

---

## Habits that build direct thinking

### 1. Narrate your life in English (silently or aloud), all day
While doing chores, walking, commuting: describe what you're doing and seeing in
simple English. "I'm waiting for the lift. It's slow today. That guy is on his
phone." Keep it *simple* – simple English thoughts, not complex Hindi thoughts
badly translated.

### 2. Label your world
For a week, mentally name objects and actions in English as you interact with them:
"kettle", "I'm plugging in the charger", "the tap is dripping". Fills gaps in
concrete daily vocab – the stuff textbooks skip.

### 3. Think in English about easy things first
Don't try to have deep philosophical thoughts in English yet. Start with:
- your to-do list
- what you'll eat
- what you did in the last hour
- simple opinions ("this coffee is too strong")

Expand to harder topics over weeks.

### 4. Learn words as images/situations, not translations
When you learn "swamped", picture yourself buried under tickets – don't store
"swamped = बहुत व्यस्त". The Hindi tag forces a translation step every time you use
it.

### 5. Use an English-English dictionary
Look up new words in a *learner's* English dictionary (Cambridge, Longman). You read
the meaning in English, see English examples. This trains English→English links.

### 6. Consume English with no subtitles in your language
Your-language subtitles keep you in translation mode. Use English subtitles, or
none. (Watching *with* English subs is fine and useful; watching with Hindi subs
trains the wrong thing.)

### 7. Keep an English-only journal (3 lines/day)
Write 3 sentences about your day in English before bed. No dictionary, no
translating – whatever you can produce directly. Speed and directness matter more
than correctness here.

### 8. Have a "no-Hindi hour"
Pick one hour a day (e.g. your commute + first hour of work thinking). In that hour,
try to keep your *inner monologue* in English. Extend it over time.

---

## When you're stuck: don't translate – simplify

If you can't say something the way you'd say it in Hindi, **say a simpler version in
English.** Don't push a complex Hindi sentence through translation.

- Want to say: "मुझे लगता है कि इस approach में scalability की दिक्कत हो सकती है
  अगर traffic अचानक बढ़ जाए।"
- Don't translate it. Say: "I think this approach might have a problem with
  scalability. If the traffic suddenly goes up, it could struggle."

Two simple English sentences > one complex translated one. This is a *skill* –
practice deliberately downgrading complexity to stay in English.

---

## Signs it's working (over 4–8 weeks)

- You catch yourself thinking in English without deciding to.
- Simple sentences come out without an intermediate step.
- You dream in English occasionally (a real, common sign).
- When you *do* get stuck, you reach for simpler English, not for Hindi.

It never becomes 100% – even advanced speakers translate for hard/emotional topics.
That's fine. Getting daily life to ~90% direct is the win.
